How to Choose Materials for a Private Label Fur & Winter Outerwear Collection

A practical comparison of fox fur, raccoon fur, leather, shearling, cashmere and faux fur—plus the specifications, sampling questions and compliance checks buyers should settle before production.

Quick answer

The right material is not simply the most expensive or the most visually dramatic. It is the material that matches your target price, garment silhouette, destination market, care expectations and order volume—and that can be identified, sampled and documented consistently.

For an efficient sourcing decision, shortlist no more than two material routes, request labelled swatches, build one approved sample, and put the final material name, colour, measurements, workmanship standard, labels and required documents into the purchase order.

01 · IdentifyUse exact material descriptions. “Premium fur” or “high-grade leather” is not a usable specification.
02 · ApproveApprove physical swatches and a sealed pre-production sample under agreed lighting and measurement methods.
03 · DocumentMake labels, test reports, origin documents and claim wording part of the buying brief—not an afterthought.

1. Start with the buying brief, not the material name

A supplier cannot recommend a reliable material from a reference photo alone. The same long coat silhouette might be developed in natural fur, shearling, wool/cashmere cloth or faux fur, but each route changes the cost structure, hand feel, weight, care method, minimum order and compliance work.

Before requesting prices, define these six points:

  1. Target customer and selling channel: luxury boutique, department store, online private label, uniform program or promotional wholesale.
  2. Target retail and landed-cost range: provide a workable range rather than asking for the “best price.”
  3. Silhouette and performance: fitted or oversized, short or full length, structured or fluid, statement volume or low bulk.
  4. Order structure: units per style, colour and size—not only the total collection quantity.
  5. Destination market: the country where the garment will be sold, because labelling, chemical, species and consumer-information requirements differ.
  6. Evidence required: material composition, origin, test scope, transaction documents, inspection standard and any customer-specific restricted-substance list.
Procurement tip: Ask the supplier to quote the same design in two clearly named material options. Compare total specifications, not just unit price. A cheaper option may require a different backing, lining, construction or care label.

3. Material-by-material sourcing notes

Fox fur: specify volume, colour and identity

Buyers often select fox fur for visual volume and long guard hair. However, “fox fur” alone is not precise enough for development. Ask the supplier to identify the material using the true animal name and any species or origin information required for your market. Then compare underfur density, guard-hair length, pelt size, colour, lustre and weight.

For dyed colours, approve a physical colour standard and define an acceptable shade range. For natural colours, agree how much variation is acceptable across panels and garments. Photographing a single sample is not a substitute for a bulk shade approval method.

Raccoon fur: confirm the exact species and market name

Raccoon-related trade names can be misunderstood across markets and languages. Do not rely on a translated catalogue name. Confirm the exact species, origin and permitted consumer-facing name for the destination country before approving labels, invoices or advertising.

From a product-development perspective, review tonal contrast, guard-hair pattern, underfur, weight, pelt matching and shedding. A hood trim and a full garment may require very different appearance and construction standards.

Leather: define the hide and finish, not just “genuine leather”

A useful leather specification includes the animal type, thickness range, surface finish, colour, grain expectation, panel-matching rule and defect tolerance. Lambskin is commonly considered when buyers want a softer drape; cowhide may be selected when a more substantial hand is required. These are general tendencies, not guarantees—the tanning and finishing process can change the result.

Agree how colour will be evaluated, especially for black and dark fashion colours. Also specify zipper, snap and buckle finish; lining composition; seam type; reinforcement; and any destination-specific restricted-substance testing agreed with the supplier or laboratory.

Shearling is not cashmere

These terms should never be combined as if they describe the same material. Shearling is sheepskin or lambskin with the wool attached to the hide. Cashmere is a fine fibre obtained from cashmere goats, spun into yarn and made into a textile. A wool/cashmere coat therefore needs a fibre-percentage and fabric specification; a shearling garment needs a skin, wool, leather-side finish and thickness specification.

This distinction matters for quotation, labels, care instructions, testing and search visibility. A page titled “cashmere shearling” without a precise explanation can confuse both buyers and search systems.

Faux fur: pile engineering determines the result

Faux fur should not be purchased from colour and price alone. Specify fibre composition, pile length, pile density or fabric weight, backing construction, width, hand feel, lustre, recovery after compression, shedding expectation and colourfastness. For hoods and cuffs, test how the pile behaves after sewing and packing; for full coats, review drape, bulk and seam appearance.

If a material is described as recycled, lower impact or environmentally preferable, request evidence that supports the exact claim and applies to the supplied material. Avoid broad “eco-friendly” language without a defined, verifiable basis.

4. What to put in the tech pack

A clear tech pack shortens the quotation cycle and makes sample comments measurable. At minimum, include:

  • Front and back flat sketches
  • Reference images labelled as reference only
  • Exact material description or requested options
  • Colour standard and shade tolerance
  • Garment measurements and tolerances
  • Size range and order ratio
  • Panel or pelt-matching direction
  • Seam and construction details
  • Lining and interlining specification
  • Zippers, snaps, hooks and other trims
  • Logo, main label, size label and care label
  • Hangtags and packaging
  • Destination country and selling channel
  • Testing and document requirements
  • Inspection level or agreed workmanship standard
  • Target sample and delivery dates
Important: If you are using a market reference image, identify which elements are inspiration and provide your own construction, measurements and branding. Do not ask a supplier to reproduce protected logos, prints or distinctive designs without authorization.

5. Sampling, MOQ and lead time: ask for separate answers

There is no responsible universal MOQ for every fur or winter outerwear style. The workable minimum depends on material availability, colour, size ratio, trim sourcing, label customization, packaging and production method. A sample MOQ, fabric or pelt minimum, and bulk garment MOQ may all be different.

Request a written development summary with:

  • sample cost and what it includes;
  • sample lead time measured from material and tech-pack confirmation;
  • bulk MOQ by style, material and colour;
  • which custom colours or trims create separate minimums;
  • bulk lead time and the event that starts the clock—for example, deposit, approved sample and confirmed materials;
  • whether sample fees are refundable or creditable, and under what written conditions;
  • shipping method, Incoterm and destination assumptions.

A precise answer such as “MOQ to be confirmed after material and colour selection” is more useful than an attractive number that does not apply to your actual style.

Quality expectations should be converted into checks that a supplier or independent inspector can perform. Use the approved sample, purchase order, tech pack and agreed test reports as the inspection baseline.

For natural materials, define how natural variation will be judged. For dyed materials, use an agreed light source and shade standard. For faux fur, specify a practical shedding and recovery evaluation. For all garments, check closures, lining attachment, label accuracy and packing condition.

7. Compliance, labelling and marketing claims

Compliance requirements depend on the material, species, destination, product category and sales channel. The buyer should confirm the applicable rules before issuing final labels or marketing copy. The following checkpoints are a starting point, not legal advice.

  • United States fur products: review the FTC requirements for the true animal name, fur origin, treatment such as dyeing or bleaching, and responsible business identity in labels, invoices and advertising.
  • European Union textiles: verify fibre-content wording and the required indication when a textile product contains non-textile parts of animal origin.
  • Species and trade controls: identify species accurately and check whether CITES or other import/export controls apply to the specific species and origin.
  • Leather and textile testing: define applicable chemical, colourfastness, physical-performance and flammability requirements with a qualified laboratory for the destination and product type.
  • Environmental claims: do not use broad claims such as “green,” “eco-friendly,” “non-toxic” or “sustainable” unless the precise claim is qualified and supported by evidence that matches the supplied product.
Claim-control rule: A certificate logo should only appear on a product page when the certificate owner, product/material scope, standard, issuer and validity can be verified. A supplier’s general certificate does not automatically certify every garment.

Official references: U.S. FTC fur labelling guidance, EU Regulation 1007/2011 on textile fibre names and labelling, CITES species checklist, and FTC Green Guides summary.

8. Ten questions to ask a potential supplier

  1. What is the exact material identification, composition, animal name or species, and origin information available for this option?
  2. Can you send individually labelled swatches for each quoted material and colour?
  3. Which parts of the reference design require construction changes for the selected material?
  4. What are the sample cost, sample lead time and revision terms?
  5. What is the bulk MOQ by style, material and colour, including any trim or dyeing minimum?
  6. Which measurements, appearance points and workmanship standards will be checked during production?
  7. How will natural variation, shade variation, panel matching and shedding be controlled?
  8. Which test reports and origin or trade documents can be supplied for this exact order?
  9. What information must be confirmed before the bulk-production lead time begins?
  10. Can the commercial invoice, labels, packaging and product description use one consistent and legally reviewed material name?

Frequently asked questions

Which material is best for a private label winter outerwear collection?

There is no universal best material. Choose according to target retail price, silhouette, destination market, care expectations and required documentation. Confirm the final choice through physical swatches and a pre-production sample.

What is the difference between shearling and cashmere?

Shearling is sheepskin or lambskin with the wool attached to the hide. Cashmere is a fine animal fibre that is spun into yarn and made into a textile. They require different specifications, labels and quality checks.

How should buyers compare fox fur and raccoon fur?

Compare verified species and origin, guard-hair length, underfur density, colour consistency, pelt size, dyeing or bleaching, shedding, weight and the intended silhouette. Use approved physical samples rather than trade names alone.

Can a supplier quote one MOQ for every style and material?

UMAY starts from 1 piece per style for samples or production orders. Material availability, colour, trims and construction still need to be confirmed in the written specification.

What should a fur or leather garment tech pack include?

Include the flat sketch, measurements and tolerances, material identification, colour reference, construction, lining, trims, branding, labels, packaging, destination country, test requirements and inspection standard.

Which quality checks should be completed before shipment?

Check approved materials and colour, measurements, workmanship, seam quality, closures and trims, lining, appearance, shedding where relevant, labels, packaging, quantity and any destination-specific reports agreed in the purchase order.

What compliance information should buyers request for natural fur?

Request the true animal name, species where needed, country of origin, whether the fur is dyed or otherwise treated, supplier identity, applicable trade documents and destination-market label information. Confirm requirements with a qualified adviser.

What information is needed for an accurate private label quotation?

Send a reference image or tech pack, material preference, quantity per style and colour, size range, branding and packaging needs, destination country, required documents and target delivery date.
